# Rules for reviewing the drafted candidates

The reviewer sees one row per merged candidate (cluster): the cell, all the cells its members came from, the month and year the passage gives (period), the model's own opinion on whether it concerns the cell's country (country_fit: yes, no, unclear; an aid for D2, never a decision), the claim (the statement itself, as the model extracted it), who spread it according to the passage, the passage's own words that call it false (verdict_words, and whether the script found them in the text), the passage quoted from the source, the share of the statement's words that are also in the passage (words_in_passage, shaded orange under 20 percent and yellow from 20 to 39: a reminder to check D4 with care, never a decision), the source documents and how many of them mention it. Each row belongs to a country and a year, its cell: the country of the reports it was drafted from, and the year its passage names (the earliest of its cells when the passage names none). The reviewer reads the claim and its passage only (not the report, the archive or fact-checking sites), writes one decision per row and, for a drop, one rule. Nothing else is edited at this stage; the wording is rewritten later, after the random draw, under the rewrite rule.

| decision | rule | definition |
|---|---|---|
| K | | None of D1 to D5 applies. |
| D | D1 | The row describes the stating or spreading of a claim (who said it, a campaign, a tactic, the image or video that carried it) instead of stating the claim. A fabricated quote is not D1: there, "X said Y" is itself the claim. |
| D | D2 | The claim involves only a country other than the row's. A claim about the row's country, a group that country belongs to (the EU, the West), or no country is not D2. |
| D | D3 | It says the same as another kept claim of any country or year. |
| D | D4 | The passage neither mentions nor implies the claim, or the claim says the opposite of a claim the passage presents as false or misleading. |
| D | D5 | Even with its passage, it is unclear what the claim says or what it is about. |

Examples from the log:
- D1. "A BJP MLA called for a boycott of Muslim vegetable vendors" (india-2020:c0018) says who said something instead of stating it. "The ruling party was allegedly using an app called Tek Fog to shape public narratives…" (india-2022:c0000) describes a tactic. "AI-generated image of Keir Starmer shared to suggest Labour no longer represents the working class" (uk-2024:c0013) describes the image that carried the claim. Not D1: "Vice President Kamala Harris said former President Donald Trump could not 'even die with dignity'" (us-2024:c0000; the passage says the audio was generated by AI): Harris never said it, so "Harris said it" is the claim. A fabricated source is the same: "A study shows that human CO2 emissions don't cause current global warming" (france-2026:c0106) and "A Dominion Voting Systems whistleblower accused US politicians of taking millions of dollars in bribes ..." (us-2026:c0001) are not D1, because the false part is what the source is said to have shown or said; a real image, video or document that carried the claim is D1.
- D2. The test is the country only: a claim is about the row's country when, as written, it names or directly involves that country, a group it belongs to (the EU, the West, Europe, NATO, the host countries of refugees) or no country at all. That a war or a crisis abroad matters to every country is not a reason to keep a claim about that country only. "S. Kasselakis and T. Macbeth naked on a beach" in the United Kingdom 2024 cell (uk-2024:c0010) is about two Greek politicians and Greece only: D2. Hong Kong belongs to China, so a claim about China is not D2 in the Hong Kong cells ("China's population is 2x OECD countries put together, so per capita emission much lower", hong-kong-2023:c0006). The review does not check when a claim circulated: the reports were collected because they describe the claims of their country and period, and a row's year is the year of its cell.
- D3. "Says the same" is the test the automatic merge applies by machine (two candidates are one claim) and that the reviewer applies to the pairs the merge missed, now in any country and any year: "mRNA vaccines can change your DNA" (us-2021:c0030) says the same as "Moderna's new potential Covid-19 vaccine will change people's DNA" (us-2019:c0007). The note names the kept row's cluster_id. "Kept" means K and used by the draw (above the first empty row of its cell, see the stopping rule): of two rows that say the same, exactly one is kept, and which one does not matter, since the claim stays in the pool either way. The import refuses a D3 row that the draw uses when its note names no such kept row (review_needs.py, d3_twin_errors).
- D4. "The Karachi port had been attacked by India" (india-2025:c0006) with the passage "Karachi port alleges this was the result of an account-level hack": the passage neither mentions nor implies the claim. "Shootings occurred during the #EndSARS Protests" (nigeria-2022:c0004) with the passage "The government denied the occurrence of shootings during the #EndSARS Protests, referred to as the #LekkiMassacre on social media, even though there were live streams of the incident": the passage presents the denial as false, and the claim says its opposite. The claim must be in its passage with its names and details: a person, an organisation, a number or a detail of what is claimed that the passage does not contain makes the row D4, also when the passage refers to it only as "he", "her", "the accused" or by another description that fits many; the row's own country may be added, as in the rewrite rule. A person, organisation or place counts as named when the passage identifies it unambiguously: by a role or title that fits only one ("his position as chair of the House Foreign Affairs Committee" is Michael McCaul, us-2024:c0006; "the electoral umpire" in Nigeria is INEC) or by a place that lies inside it ("the Rangia conflict" is in Assam, india-2026:c0006); a place gives the region it lies in, not a part of it ("Kaduna state" does not give "southern Kaduna", nigeria-2023:c0024). Examples: "George Soros funded the Central American caravan" with the passage "he was falsely said to have financed the Central American caravan" (us-2018:c0031); "Injecting disinfectant is a treatment for coronavirus" with a passage about people who "ingested cleaning fluids" (us-2020:c0003). The same holds for a place or a date that only says where or when: "London has 'no-go' areas" with a passage that lists "claims about 'no-go' areas" and never names London (uk-2025:c0001) is D4. A passage that denies a claim mentions it, whoever the passage is and whether or not it is right: "The proposed legislation would provide the ACMA with content removal powers" with the passage "The proposed legislation would not provide the ACMA with content removal powers ..." (australia-2024:c0004) is not D4, because the passage corrects that very claim; a row that states the passage's denial instead says the opposite of a claim the passage presents as false, D4. Not D4: "The #LekkiMassacre did not occur during the #EndSARS Protests" (nigeria-2021:c0010, the same passage, a row not yet reviewed; the denial itself); "The vaccine alters DNA" (nigeria-2021:c0003, passage "Messages included false claims … that the vaccine alters DNA"); "A cancer hospital would not accept new Muslim patients until they provided proof that they have tested negative for Covid-19" (india-2020:c0021, passage "A cancer hospital in the country also put out a notice stating that they would not accept new Muslim patients…": the passage mentions it and presents nothing as false). The verdict of the passage is used only to find which side of a reported dispute is the claim; it never decides whether a row is true. A passage that is only the title of a cited source (an endnote or a reference; the reviewer, 2026-11-05, in the rewrite check) is not the report's own words: the report says what the sentence citing it says, so the row is kept when that sentence, or the text around it, mentions or implies the claim (the title may still give its exact words and numbers), and is D4 otherwise. Applied to the 15 such rows of the main set: D4 for "Obama sold vote processing rights to SCYTL" (us-2018:c0016, cited only for 'the same falsehood was spread during the 2016 and 2012 election campaigns', the Soros voting machines), "President Trump was secretly winking at QAnon" (us-2020:c0005), "Ilhan Omar partied on the 9/11 anniversary" (us-2018:c0039, cited for 'smeared Democratic members of Congress') and the Tuskegee post (us-2021:c0058, cited with 'See, e.g.' for vaccine narratives that never say anyone was injected); the other 11 stay, because the report's own text states their claim. Rows found later to be titles (2026-11-06; the first search looked for web addresses and outlet names, the second for where each passage sits in its report): D4 for "Labour no longer represents the working class" (uk-2024:c0007, endnote 8 of its report, cited in its Table 1 only for AI-generated 'images' of candidates; decided by the reviewer, who read the report again).
- D5. "The attacker was a Muslim migrant new to the UK" (uk-2024:c0000) with a passage that never says which attack: what the claim says is clear, what it is about is not. A precise claim stays K even when it is technical, general ("Voter fraud is widespread and election integrity is compromised"; the row this example comes from, us-2022:c0009, was found D4 in the rewrite check of 2026-11-05, because its passage never says that voter fraud is widespread; the example still shows that a general claim is not D5) or an opinion. Decided in the rewrite check (2026-11-05, the reviewer): "President Barack Obama himself was illegal, and therefore treasonous" (us-2016:c0013) is D5, because 'illegal' does not say what he is claimed to be; "The Democratic Party assisted voters in Texas and elsewhere in being represented in voter rolls or voting multiple times" (us-2016:c0006) is D5, because its passage has no subject and the report's own word for them, 'illegals', does not say who they are; "Rashida Tlaib is guilty by association with Islamic organizations or other Muslims such as activist Linda Sarsour" (us-2018:c0021) is D5, because 'guilty by association' describes a smear and never says what she is claimed to have done; "Donald Trump is a sacrificial demigod" (us-2020:c0040) is D5, a portrayal whose content the report never gives; "Polling places are closed, that polling has ended or that votes are not being counted" (us-2020:c0121) is D5, because its passage is a category of Twitter's election policy quoted in the report's appendix, not a claim the report says circulated.
- The mirror case of D4: when the passage mentions a claim and the row states its opposite, the row is D4, by the second half when the passage presents the claim as false ("Shootings occurred during the #EndSARS Protests", nigeria-2022:c0004), and by the first half when the passage only reports or states it ("The Pennsylvania postal worker was not retaliated against by the USPS" with a passage that reports the claim that he was, us-2020:c0111). It does not work the other way: a passage that denies a claim names it, a passage that states a claim does not name its opposite.
- One rule is recorded per drop: when two or more rules apply, any of them may be recorded; the only condition is that the recorded rule applies. So the counts per rule are not exclusive.

Conditions of the review, which the paper states: every decision is logged with its rule and released; after the draw and before any rewrite, every decision the draw uses was read again against the rules above and the decisions that contradicted them were corrected, each with its reason in its note (see "Check against the rules" below); the review happens before any system is run on real queries and without looking at the archive; the reviewer filters the pool but does not choose the queries, the seeded random draw does; no blind second review is made (decided by the reviewer on 2026-10-30: the time goes to the final set of narratives); the paper states it as a limitation; precedent: the TREC 2023 Deep Learning track filtered its queries by NIST assessors, who "removed the queries that do not look reasonable" (13 of 48 T5 queries, 18 of 49 GPT-4 queries, 51 of 147 real queries kept), and its overview reports no agreement figure for that filtering; what is lost: no measure of whether another person reads the rules the same way, and a wrong D silently removes a narrative from the pool, which no later test of the final set can see; review_needs.py second and agreement stay in the code and in the toy test.

Stopping rule (from 2026-10-30): the rows of every cell are put in a seeded random order (review_needs.py sheet --allocation, seed 20261030), and the reviewer reviews each cell from its top until it holds its number of queries plus 2 K rows, or until its rows run out; only the rows from the top down to the first empty row are used by the draw. Reason: the draw needs only that many K rows per cell, and the first K rows of a random order are a random sample of all the cell's K rows (inverse sampling, Haldane 1945), so the draw stays a random draw from all kept rows while the review shrinks from 1,504 rows to about 600. The 565 decisions made before the random order existed (in the order of the source documents) were carried into the new sheet; those that fall below the first empty row of their cell are kept in the log but not used.

Top-up targets (from 2026-11-03): a year with too few kept rows is filled from the kept rows its country's other years leave, so in a country whose years with no row left are short of queries, the shortfall (net of the spare kept rows those years already hold) is added to the targets of the country's years that still have rows, in proportion to their rows; review_needs.py computes it.

Check against the rules (2026-11-04, after the draw and before any rewrite): every decided row the draw uses, and the decided rows below a gap that the cells still to review will bring into use, is read again against the rules above; the rows whose decision contradicts them are listed, the reviewer decides each one, and every change carries its reason in the row's note. Earlier corrections (2026-10-30 and 2026-11-03) are tagged in their notes in the same way and listed in WORK/drafting/consistency_pass_2026-11-03.txt and consistency_pass_2026-11-03_round3.txt. The draw is run again with the same seed on the corrected log: there is no replacement rule, because a dropped row that should have been kept can only enter through the draw.

Rewrite rules (after the draw; draw_queries.py rewrite-sheet and import-rewrite; written on 2026-11-04, before any query was rewritten; W1 and W2 reworded, W2 widened to the whole report and W5 added on 2026-11-05, after two pilot rows had been read and before any import; later the same day, still before any import, W2 given 'as far as the passage or the report gives them' and W5 allowed to be reworded):

| rule | definition |
|---|---|
| W1 | The query states the reviewed claim. It may be reworded or repunctuated. Apart from what W2 allows, nothing is added and nothing is left out (except under W4). |
| W2 | The query can be understood without its passage: a reader who sees only the query can tell which claim it is and who, which event, where and when it is about, as far as the passage or the report gives them. To that end it may say who, which event, where and when, taken only from the passage or from what the report says about the same claim, and only as much as is needed: a name rather than a description. |
| W3 | A place, the name of an event or a date enters the sentence only when the claim cannot be understood without it. |
| W4 | When the reviewed claim joins two or more claims and a fact-check of one would not check another, the query states one of them, chosen at random with a published seed. |
| W5 | When a judge might not know who or what a name in the query is, the query carries a background that says it, written only from what the report says; it may be reworded, and the report's words it was written from go into the context column. The judges see it; no system searches it, the writers do not see it, and it is not part of the claim. |

How they are read: W1 makes "X said that", "a video shows", "posts claim" and a verdict word ("false", "fake", "AI-generated") wrong, since they add to the claim or replace it, except where the reviewed claim is itself a fabricated quote or source (D1). W2 allows the query's country, its adjective or abbreviation, a month and a year of the period without any context. Test of W2 (the reviewer, 2026-11-05): read against the whole report, the final query is not D4; and every added word is needed (cover it: if who and which event stay clear, delete it). W1 and W2 were reworded on 2026-11-05 (before: "nothing is added to what is claimed", which read as forbidding what W2 allows) and W2 names its reader. W5 (2026-11-05, the reviewer, on pilot-02, before any import) follows the TREC topics, which keep the need statement apart from the text submitted to the system and carry a narrative written for the assessors (Harman 1994, Overview of the Second Text REtrieval Conference (TREC-2), NIST SP 500-215, section 3.3, pp. 4-5: "keeping the topic (the need statement) distinct from the query (the actual text submitted to the system)" and "There is a narrative section which is aimed at providing a complete description of document relevance for the assessors"; in TREC-2 the systems could still build their queries from the whole topic, narrative included, p. 5; Sanderson 2010, Foundations and Trends in IR 4(4), section 3, p. 277: a title "which could be viewed as the type of query that might be submitted to a search engine" and a narrative "of what documents the searcher would consider as relevant"), and most closely the TREC 2022 Health Misinformation track, whose background the assessors read and the automatic runs could not use (Clarke, Maistro, Seifikar, Smucker 2022, Overview of the TREC 2022 Health Misinformation Track (Notebook), NIST SP 500-338, section 1, p. 1: "Each topic is also supplied with a background providing additional clarification to the assessors" and "Automatic runs could only make use of the topic's question or query"; Figure 1, p. 2, shows a topic with its background; the track page trec-health-misinfo.github.io, read 2026-11-05, says the same: a background field "that will give basic background information on the topic's question", "The NIST assessors will be provided the topic's question and background", "Automatic runs may not use the background, evidence, or the answer fields"). VERIFIED in both papers on 2026-11-05: papers/added-2026-09-10/harman-1994-trec2-overview.txt (NIST's text of the proceedings) and papers/added-2026-09-10/trec2022-health-misinfo-overview.pdf; the paper cites them as harman1994trec2 and clarke2022hm. Differences, stated so that nobody reads more into the analogy: the TREC backgrounds were written by the organisers, ours are written only from the report, and the report's words they come from are kept in the context column; the TREC 2022 restriction is stated for automatic runs only, while here nothing that searches sees the background, the writers included. Why: a fact-checked claim may name a person by a role ("Kavanaugh's accuser") and a judge who does not know who the person is could not match it, while a description inside the query would add a second story to the searched sentence ("who testified that he had assaulted her"). Until later on 2026-11-05 the background was the report's words copied word for word; the reviewer then allowed it to be reworded, before any import. The report's words it was written from go into the context column, which import-rewrite checks word for word against the report; every name and year of the background must be in the passage, the claim or the context, and it may carry no verdict word, since the judges are not told what the fact-checkers decided; the guideline tells the judges that it is not part of the narrative. The query and its background may be reworded (W1, W5); what is copied word for word is only the context column, the report's words that the query and the background were written from, so that a reader can find them in the report. Terms of the claim (the reviewer, 2026-11-06, on us-2018-14, before any import): the query keeps the claim's own term for the people it is about ('illegals', 'illegal aliens'), because it is part of what circulated and what fact-checks quote (narrowed by the reviewer later on 2026-11-06, on us-2019-10, before any import: the reading is about the words for people; another word of the claim may be reworded under W1 when its passage explains it, as 'the blood of Christ' became 'the belief in God', from the passage's 'the “blood of Christ,” or a belief in God'); the terms are not made uniform across queries, since a uniform term would often be a word the claim's report never uses; when the report explains the term, the background gives the report's words ('‘Illegals’: undocumented immigrants', from 'false claims about undocumented immigrants voting'); when it does not, the claim is D5 if the term leaves unclear who or what it is about (us-2016:c0006). Details of the claim (the reviewer, 2026-11-06, on us-2016-03, before any import): W1's 'nothing is left out' is about what the claim says; a sentence of the claim that only gives details of what another of its sentences already says may be left out when the message stays the same ('Hank Aaron’s tragic death is part of a wave of suspicious deaths among elderly closely following administration of COVID vaccines' carries the message, that the vaccine killed him; 'The baseball legend received the Moderna vaccine on Jan. 5, in an attempt to inspire other Black Americans to step up to the plate and get the vaccine' only gives the date and the purpose of that vaccination). W2 and elections (the reviewer, 2026-11-05, on us-2016-01, before any import): a claim about what happens in an election (fraud, ballots, voter rolls, results, a campaign, the vote of a group) says which election, as the passage or the report gives it ('In the 2016 election, ...'), because the guideline treats a dated episode of another year as a different claim; a claim about how an electoral system works (Australia's preferences, the AEC's powers, a 'vote of no confidence' formula) is not about one election and names none; when the report never says which election (nigeria-2021-01: 'ahead of the election'), the query cannot say it either. W2's 'as far as the passage or the report gives them' (added later on 2026-11-05) does not limit the wording, only the content: when the report does not give who, which event, where or when, the query does not say it (pilot-09: the report never says which bankruptcy laws changed or when, so the query cannot say it either). W2 said "the same page of the report" until 2026-11-05; the reviewer widened it to the whole report that day, after reading two pilot rows and before any import, because the drafting model extracted the passage from the whole report, a paragraph can run over two pages, and a passage can be a note whose story is told in the body (pilot-02: the endnote gives "Christine Ford", the body "Christine Blasey Ford"). What stays narrow: only who, which event, where and when, only as the report says them about the same claim, never new content (W1), and every word taken is copied into the context column, released and checked against the report. W4, tested on the drawn queries: "Corbyn did not condemn bombings by the IRA and called for the abolition of the army" joins two claims (a fact-check of one does not check the other); "The official account of the Nazi Holocaust is a lie and the number of Jews killed has been exaggerated" is one claim (one fact-check of Holocaust denial checks both). The test is the relevance guideline's (pool/GUIDELINE.md, line 8): a claim about the same people or the same topic that says something else is a different claim, even with the same intent (its example: "vaccines contain microchips" and "vaccines cause infertility"); so "Hillary Clinton is a traitor! Hillary Clinton is a liar! Hillary Clinton is insane!" joins three claims, although all three attack the same person. Without W4 the judge could not answer the guideline's question for a fact-check that matches one part only. The reviewer writes the claims in the sheet's parts column ("first || second"), in the order the reviewed claim states them (the pick depends only on the seed, the query and the number of parts, so the order must not be the reviewer's choice either), and decides every W4 row before the first import; the parts are fixed at the import that shows the pick (import-rewrite remembers them in <out>.w4.json and flags any later change, so a split cannot be removed or reordered after the pick is known); import-rewrite says which part the query keeps, chosen with random.Random("<draw seed>:part:<query id>"), records the parts and the part kept in the provenance, and warns when the text looks closer to another part. W4 and D3 (the reviewer, 2026-11-06, before any import): the part a split query keeps can say the same as another query (uk-2019-01 and india-2024-07 both have the part 'Muslims are violent'); that is a D3 found after the pick, so the query is replaced under the replacement rule, the split one, or the later one in the query file when both were split; import-rewrite reports two queries whose statements have the same words, and the reviewer reads the picks for claims that say the same in other words. Quotation marks (the reviewer, 2026-11-06, before any import): a query has no quotation marks, because a person who searches does not type them; W1 allows repunctuation, so they are removed and the words kept ('could not even die with dignity'), and a quoted text is given as what it says when that reads better ('a billboard saying that pedos ain’t that bad'); BM25 ignores them in any case, the neural models would see them; a background may keep them, since no system searches it; import-rewrite flags any quotation mark in a query. Hyphens and dashes (the reviewer, later on 2026-11-06, before any import, for the same reason): a hyphen becomes a space ('COVID 19', 'pro Pakistan', 'ZANU PF'), which BM25 reads as it read the hyphen, and a word reworded where a space would not read ('rerun', '9 to 10 May'); commas, full stops and apostrophes stay, because the judges and the writers read the query as a sentence; import-rewrite flags a hyphen or a dash in a query. The 30-word limit and the check against copying the passage, used until 2026-11-04 before any rewrite, were dropped: neither had a basis in the literature or the data, a long claim is still one claim, and many passages are the claim itself.

Conditions of the rewrite: no query is dropped or split; the rewrite is done, like the review, without looking at the archive or at any system's results. Replacement. Only a query that breaks a review rule (D1 to D5) is replaced. Its decision is corrected in the review sheet. Then one new query is drawn at random, with its own seed, from the kept rows of the same country and year that are not already used. If the old query had filled a short year, or no unused kept row of that year is left, the new one is drawn from the same country instead. If the country has none left either, the query is removed and the paper reports the shortfall. No other query changes.

How it is done: draw_queries.py replace, seed "<draw seed>:replace:<query id>:<n>" (n = the earlier replacements of that query); the old query file is kept beside the new one, every replacement is logged in WORK/drafting/replacements.jsonl, and the rewrite sheet is rebuilt with rewrite-sheet --keep, which keeps the work of every other row. Why it keeps a random sample: removing a row that should never have been kept and drawing one at random from the remaining kept rows gives the same chances as if that row had been dropped before the draw.

Procedure of the rewrite: A local model proposes the statement and, as a hint, the place and the date the passage gives (propose_rewrites.py, prompt queries/prompts/rewrite_needs.txt; a place or a date the passage does not name is dropped), and the reviewer accepts or corrects every row after reading its passage (since later on 2026-11-05 the sheet no longer shows the local model's proposal, at the reviewer's request: free_text holds the closed model's suggestion, and the local model's proposals stay in their file, which import-rewrite --proposals records). Since 2026-11-05 (the reviewer, after writing three pilot rows and before any import) a closed model also proposes every row not yet written: it reads the report around the passage under these rules and writes the sentence, the context, the background and the W4 parts, each row with its reason (draw_queries.py rewrite-sheet --closed-proposals WORK/drafting/rewrite_closed_<queries>.jsonl; the reasons stay in that file, the sheet shows only the suggestion; pilot-01, pilot-02 and pilot-05 were written by the reviewer before and are not the closed model's, except their context, which the closed model filled at the reviewer's request and the file records as context_only); the reviewer accepts or corrects every row; import-rewrite --closed-proposals keeps the closed model's proposal beside the final text and counts the changes. The closed model knows more than the reports say: the import catches a name or a year that it brings in from elsewhere, and only the reviewer's reading catches the rest; the closed model's proposals cannot be regenerated, unlike the local model's, and are released as made. import-rewrite checks the final text (names and years from elsewhere, verdict words, attribution, and the W4 part) and counts the changed proposals. The passage is only the piece the drafting cut from the report, and what passed the source conditions is the whole report, so the reviewer may open the report (the sheet gives its file, the PDF page and the link) when the passage leaves unclear who, where, or when for a claim about something that happened at a time; the whole report may be read, but only what it says about the same claim is used; only who, where and when may be taken from it, never new content of the claim; when the text then carries a name or a year that is in neither the passage, the claim nor the period, the words of the report that give it are copied word for word into the sheet's context column (the import lists every such word); since later on 2026-11-05 the context also holds the report's words that the rest of the rewording and the background were written from, for every row that uses the report beyond its passage (pieces joined by "…", for example "Election Week 2016 … November 7, 2016"); a date taken from the report enters the sentence only under the rule above and never turns when a claim was spread into what the claim says ("Barack Obama did not trust Hillary Clinton at the 2016 elections" is wrong: the report says the narrative was spread on 7 November 2016, not that the distrust was in 2016). Nothing may come from elsewhere (memory, Wikipedia, the fact-checks). import-rewrite checks that every name and year of the text is in the passage, the claim, the draft or the context (a year also in the period), that the context is in the report word for word (--reports), and releases the context as provenance.rewrite_context. The review reads only the passage (D5), so the drawn rows already say, from their passage, what they are about; the report is opened only to write that in one self-contained sentence.

No brackets (decided 2026-11-03, before any row was rewritten, on the question why the queries would carry where and when if every fact-check about the claim is relevant): the query is the claim as one self-contained sentence and carries a place or a date only when it cannot be understood without them or they name the event the claim is about (which attack, which election). Reasons: (1) relevance does not depend on them: pool/GUIDELINE.md ("other names, another number, another country or another date" may differ; for a query about one event the same event counts in any year, another event of the same kind does not), so a place or a date matters only to say which event; (2) appended to every query they would be search words for BM25 and the dense pipeline that no relevance decision uses, words the formula writers would have to be told to ignore, and an invitation to the judges to reject fact-checks from another country or year against the guideline; (3) the literature writes the query as the claim itself: Singh, Scarton and Bontcheva 2023, EPJ Data Science 12:59, abstract ("a misinformation claim is used as a query"); Upravitelev et al. 2026 (arXiv 2603.22015), section 1 and appendix A.4 (the narrative's "core message", for example "Global warming is not happening. Weather is cold/snowing"); and fact-checkers restate a claim to make it "clearer, context-independent, and self-contained" (Shaar et al. 2020, ACL, section 3, PDF page 4), which is the form of the claims in the archive; none appends a place or a date. The country and the year of a query stay in the query file (stratum, source, provenance): they say where the query comes from (the sampling design), not what is searched for. Example: "Barack Obama did not trust Hillary Clinton." stays as it is (under line 7 a fact-check of the claim from 2008 or 2016 is relevant). The model's prompt is unchanged (it already tells the model to put a place or a date in the statement only when they are part of the claim, so the saved replies stay valid); propose_rewrites.py no longer appends the place and the date and shows them to the reviewer as a hint column.
